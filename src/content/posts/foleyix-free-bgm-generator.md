Some projects need a song. Others need a musical bed that leaves room for the picture, narration, or play itself. Foleyix’s new [free BGM generator](https://foleyix.com/bgm-generator) is built for the second job: describe an instrumental cue, shape the direction in plain language, then listen to the result and download it from the same page.

The useful part is how much of the direction stays in your hands. Rather than choosing a locked preset and hoping it fits, you can start with a style, add a few musical details, and edit the resulting description before generation. The page says the submitted text is used as written, with no hidden instructions added behind the prompt.

## Start with a style, then make it yours

The style shortcuts cover familiar starting points such as lo-fi, piano, cinematic, ambient, jazz, acoustic folk, synthwave, electronic, Chinese instrumental, playful orchestra, suspense, and corporate music. They help answer the broad first question: what kind of arrangement should this cue lean toward?

The tags add more specific direction. You can combine a mood such as calm, hopeful, tense, or nostalgic with a use such as study, film, game, travel, café, or product video, then add instrumentation such as piano, strings, guitar, synthesizer, or guzheng. The tags are visible words in the music description. They can be edited or removed, so a “cinematic” starting point can become a quiet piano cue, a lighter travel piece, or something more particular to the scene.

That editable prompt is useful when a soundtrack is close but not quite right. “Warm and calm” is a mood; “fingerpicked acoustic guitar, warm bass, and a light shaker for a sunny travel diary” gives the generator a more concrete arrangement and use case. You can change the instruments, energy, or setting in the text itself instead of treating a preset as a final answer.

## From prompt to usable cue

The workflow is straightforward: choose a starting style, add or edit the words that describe the mood and arrangement, generate the cue, and audition it. The gallery offers a second way to learn: begin with a sample close to the scene, inspect its prompt, and use that language as a reference for your own direction. A concrete prompt can name the instruments, tempo, energy, setting, and how the ending should feel.

Because the generator is focused on instrumental BGM, it fits a different place in a production than a voice or a full song. A video editor might use a cue under footage; a podcast producer can create a bed that leaves space for speech; a game maker can explore a mood before settling on a loop or a longer score. If the project also needs narration or video, Foleyix can sit alongside tools such as [ElevenLabs](/tool/elevenlabs) and [Runway](/tool/runway); for song-first music exploration, [Suno](/tool/suno) is another point of reference.

To try the presets, browse the samples, or open the generator, visit [Foleyix’s free BGM generator](https://foleyix.com/bgm-generator).
